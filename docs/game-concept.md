# Living Story World

## Game Concept & Mechanics Specification

### 1. Purpose

Create a highly engaging game for a four-year-old that feels primarily like imaginative play and adventure, while deliberately developing useful cognitive, educational, social and practical skills.

The game should be enjoyable enough that the child actively chooses it over conventional children's tablet games, without relying on addictive engagement mechanics.

The game should also occasionally move the child's attention away from the screen and into the real world through physical actions, observation, conversation and interaction with parents.

This document describes the intended player experience, game structure and mechanics only. It deliberately does not prescribe technology or implementation.

---

# 2. Core Concept

The child chooses a magical character who becomes the main character of the game and their persistent in-game friend.

The child helps this character explore a growing world, solve problems, play with things, meet other characters and take part in stories.

The child is not primarily represented by an avatar inside the world. Instead, the main character occasionally speaks directly to the child and asks for their help.

The game should feel like:

> **"My magical friend and I are having adventures together."**

rather than:

> "I am completing educational exercises."

The world should eventually be capable of incorporating characters, games, jokes and stories from the child's real imagination and family life.

Examples include:

- Crocodile River
- Floor Is Lava
- an imaginary worm called Wormly
- imaginary monsters
- family stories
- favourite activities
- drawings and creations
- parent-created stories

These personalised elements should feel like part of the same coherent world rather than separate mini-games.

---

# 3. Player

Primary player:

**Age:** approximately 4–5 years old.

The game should assume:

- little or no reading ability
- no requirement to spell
- basic number recognition and counting may already be strong
- touch-screen familiarity
- limited ability to use conventional game controllers or keyboards
- emerging ability to follow multi-step instructions
- emerging working memory
- developing reasoning, sequencing and pattern recognition
- short or variable concentration span
- strong imaginative play
- enjoyment of repetition
- willingness to replay favourite activities many times

The game must be independently usable after minimal introduction.

---

# 4. Player Promise

When the child opens the game, they should expect:

> **Something interesting is happening in my friend's world, and I can help decide what happens next.**

The game should provide:

- exploration
- characters
- humour
- surprise
- movement
- creative play
- problems worth solving
- repeatable activities
- occasional interaction with the real world

Education should emerge naturally from those things.

---

# 5. Design Pillars

## 5.1 The child can play independently

The game must not depend on reading.

Instructions should primarily use:

- character speech
- animation
- demonstration
- clear visual cues
- simple symbols

Interactions should be forgiving and discoverable.

The child should rarely need an adult to explain what to press next.

---

## 5.2 Learning is part of the play

Educational activities must have a reason to exist within the world.

Avoid:

> "Which of these is biggest?"

Prefer:

> The character needs a plank long enough to cross a gap.

Avoid:

> "Remember these three objects."

Prefer:

> The character shows the child what is needed for a picnic, then the child travels somewhere and has to find those items from memory.

Whenever possible, the learning activity should cause something meaningful to happen in the story or world.

---

## 5.3 The game must still be fun without the lesson

The game should contain intrinsically enjoyable activities such as:

- navigating
- jumping
- cake making
- cooking
- decorating
- painting
- dressing characters
- dancing
- potion making
- collecting
- exploring
- interacting with funny characters

Some activities should be worth replaying simply because the activity itself is enjoyable.

Educational challenges can be layered into these activities without defining them completely.

---

## 5.4 The world belongs to the child

The game should gradually feel increasingly personal.

Over time it should be possible to incorporate:

- the child's chosen main character
- characters invented by the child
- characters from family games and stories
- places inspired by family imagination
- things the child has created
- adventures suggested by the child or parent

Wormly, for example, is not the game's central mascot. Wormly is simply one possible recurring character from this particular child's imagination.

---

## 5.5 The screen occasionally reaches into the real world

The game should occasionally require something that cannot be completed purely through screen tapping.

Examples:

- jumping
- spinning
- standing tall
- crouching
- dancing
- finding an object
- observing something nearby
- looking outside
- asking a parent a question
- showing the character a favourite toy
- explaining something to another person

These moments are called **World Bridges**.

They should feel like part of the adventure rather than interruptions for exercise or education.

---

## 5.6 The world must be expandable

The game should support adding:

- new characters
- new places
- new stories
- new educational concepts
- new toys
- new World Bridges

without changing the fundamental player experience.

The long-term intention is that personalised adventures could eventually be created rapidly, potentially with AI assistance.

---

# 6. Anti-Pillars

The game should not become:

- a collection of disconnected educational mini-games
- a worksheet disguised with cartoon graphics
- dependent on reading
- primarily a reflex/action game
- highly competitive
- punishment-heavy
- dependent on points, coins or streaks
- designed to maximise time-on-device
- an endless feed of content
- an open-ended AI chatbot
- a virtual friend designed to replace real social interaction
- a complex open-world game requiring camera controls or sophisticated navigation

---

# 7. Core Player Relationship

The child's chosen character is the persistent protagonist.

The child is their helper, friend and co-adventurer.

The character can occasionally acknowledge the child directly.

Examples:

> "Can you help me?"

> "I can't remember which one we need."

> "Look what I found!"

> "Can you find something like this in your house?"

> "What do you think we should do?"

The character should not constantly talk.

Periods of autonomous exploration and experimentation are important.

The relationship should remain clearly within the framing of a game character.

---

# 8. Core Game Loop

The primary loop is:

## Explore → Notice → Help → Change → Continue

### Explore

The child moves the main character around the environment and interacts with interesting objects.

### Notice

Something attracts attention.

For example:

- a character wants help
- an object is moving
- a path is blocked
- something is missing
- something unusual has happened

### Help

The child performs an activity.

This may involve:

- reasoning
- remembering
- navigating
- selecting
- arranging
- creating
- moving physically
- interacting with another person

### Change

The child's action has a visible consequence.

Examples:

- a bridge opens
- a character becomes happy
- rain starts
- a cake is completed
- an object appears
- a crocodile moves
- a party begins

### Continue

The child can either:

- continue the current adventure
- replay an enjoyable activity
- explore somewhere else

The game should avoid trapping the child in rigid sequential levels wherever possible.

---

# 9. Two Types of Play

Activities should broadly fall into two categories.

## Challenges

Challenges require the child to think, remember, decide or act.

Examples:

- remembering an object
- choosing something by size
- completing a pattern
- deciding which object will solve a problem
- remembering a sequence
- crossing a hazard
- finding something in the real world

Challenges usually have a goal.

## Toys

Toys are activities that are enjoyable to repeat without a completion state.

Examples:

- cake making
- painting
- dressing up
- potion making
- dancing
- music
- decorating
- arranging objects

A toy may optionally contain educational prompts, but these should not prevent free play.

An adventure can lead naturally into a toy.

Example:

The character needs a birthday cake.

The player helps obtain ingredients.

The child then enters the cake-making toy and can make the cake however they want.

Afterwards, they can immediately make another.

---

# 10. Learning Framework

Learning should extend beyond basic counting, colours and recognising letters.

Initial areas should include:

### Working memory

Remember:

- objects
- attributes
- short sequences
- instructions
- routes

### Comparison and measurement

Understand:

- bigger / smaller
- taller / shorter
- longer / shorter
- more / fewer
- same / different
- fits / doesn't fit

### Patterns

Recognise and create:

- AB patterns
- AAB patterns
- ABC patterns
- missing elements
- incorrect elements

### Spatial reasoning

Understand:

- over
- under
- behind
- in front
- between
- inside
- outside
- relative position
- fitting shapes and objects together

### Sequencing

Understand order within:

- recipes
- routines
- stories
- physical movements
- journeys
- processes

### Number relationships

Progress beyond simple counting into:

- equal groups
- more/fewer
- one more/one less
- simple sharing
- combining small quantities
- recognising quantity without always counting

Formal arithmetic notation is not initially required.

### Language and pre-reading

Explore:

- rhyming
- sounds within words
- initial sounds
- syllables
- vocabulary
- listening comprehension
- describing
- predicting
- storytelling

### Attention and executive function

Practise:

- waiting
- watching
- remembering rules
- changing rules
- resisting an incorrect action
- focusing on relevant information
- planning before acting

### Social reasoning

Explore:

- recognising emotions
- helping characters
- turn-taking
- considering what another character needs
- resolving simple conflicts

### Practical life knowledge

Explore concepts such as:

- routines
- washing
- brushing teeth
- sleep
- food
- getting dressed
- tidying
- weather
- seasons
- hot/cold
- materials
- simple cause and effect

These should be taught through situations rather than lectures.

---

# 11. Difficulty

Difficulty should change by altering the thinking required, not merely making actions faster.

For example, a retrieval activity might progress:

### Stage 1

"Bring me the teddy."

### Stage 2

"Bring me the small teddy."

### Stage 3

"Bring me the small teddy with the blue hat."

### Stage 4

"Bring me something round and yellow."

### Stage 5

"Bring me the apple, then the blue cup."

### Stage 6

"Bring me something that will keep us dry."

This allows one activity to develop from recognition through memory, language, sequencing and reasoning.

The child should rarely experience explicit difficulty labels.

---

# 12. Failure

Failure must be safe, funny and recoverable.

Avoid:

- lives
- game over screens
- losing collected rewards
- harsh sounds
- shame
- long resets

Prefer:

- bouncing
- splashing
- silly character reactions
- gentle correction
- returning a short distance
- being invited to try again

Errors can occasionally produce amusing outcomes.

The emotional goal is:

> "Oops! Again!"

rather than frustration.

---

# 13. Rewards

The primary reward should be the consequence of play.

Examples:

- something opens
- something funny happens
- the world changes
- a character reacts
- an activity becomes available
- an object is used
- a celebration happens

Persistent cosmetic items and world changes can also provide rewards.

Avoid relying heavily on:

- coins
- stars
- random prizes
- daily streaks
- timed rewards
- engagement pressure

The game should encourage intrinsic motivation.

---

# 14. Real-World Bridges

A World Bridge temporarily connects the game to the child's physical environment.

World Bridges should be occasional rather than constant.

They fall into four categories.

## Move

Examples:

- jump three times
- stomp
- spin
- crouch
- stretch
- copy a dance
- freeze

## Find

Examples:

- find something soft
- find something round
- find something bigger than your hand
- find something that rolls
- find two things of different sizes

## Observe

Examples:

- look outside and identify the weather
- determine whether it is light or dark
- listen for different sounds
- find something made from a particular material

## Connect

Examples:

- ask a parent a question
- show someone something
- tell someone what happened
- ask someone to perform an animal sound
- complete an activity together

The result should feed back into the game.

Example:

The character needs something soft for a picnic.

The child finds a cushion in the real world.

The character then searches for a cushion in their world.

---

# 15. Parent Interaction

Parent involvement should enhance rather than gate normal play.

Most of the game must remain playable independently.

Occasional prompts can encourage meaningful interaction.

Examples:

> "Ask a grown-up what their favourite animal was when they were little."

> "Can someone make an animal noise for us?"

> "Tell someone the three things we found."

> "Teach someone our dance."

These activities can support:

- conversation
- memory
- family connection
- storytelling
- confidence
- explaining ideas

---

# 16. Stories and Adventures

The persistent world is the primary game environment.

Stories are adventures that temporarily introduce:

- a problem
- characters
- goals
- events
- changes to the world

Stories should not require the player to follow every step rigidly.

The child should often remain free to explore or become distracted by something else.

Stories may eventually be represented as books within the world.

Opening a book can begin an adventure.

Completed adventures may later be replayed or retold as personalised stories based on what the child actually did.

This is a future extension rather than a requirement of the first prototype.

---

# 17. Personalisation

The main character should eventually support meaningful customisation.

Potential attributes include:

- creature type
- appearance
- clothes
- accessories
- name

Possible character styles include:

- unicorn
- mermaid
- witch
- cat
- cute monster
- other fantasy creatures

The character should remain consistent across adventures.

Additional characters may come from:

- authored game content
- parent ideas
- child-created ideas
- family stories
- drawings
- future generated content

---

# 18. Initial Prototype Adventure

## Crocodile River

The purpose of the prototype is to test the fundamental game experience.

### Setting

A small environment divided by a river.

It contains:

- the main character
- a starting area
- Crocodile River
- rocks or stepping stones
- crocodiles
- an area containing objects on the far side
- a destination/activity area on the starting side

---

# 19. Crocodile River Core Mechanic

The main character needs an item from the other side of the river.

The child is shown or told what the character needs.

The information then disappears.

The child must:

1. remember the item
2. navigate the river
3. avoid crocodiles
4. reach the opposite side
5. identify the correct item amongst distractors
6. pick it up
7. carry it back across the river
8. deliver it

The retrieved object should then be used for something meaningful.

---

# 20. River Crossing

The character crosses by jumping between safe rocks.

The child chooses where to jump.

Crocodiles create a mild hazard.

They may:

- occupy spaces
- move slowly
- open/close their mouths
- temporarily block routes

Landing on a crocodile produces a funny reaction such as:

- bouncing
- splashing
- crocodile noise

The player is returned a short distance.

Failure should not erase substantial progress.

The crossing should itself be fun enough that deliberately jumping on crocodiles remains amusing.

---

# 21. Retrieval Challenge

Initially, the player sees the desired object before crossing.

Example:

A small purple bag with a yellow star.

The item is no longer displayed when the child reaches the other side.

Several objects are available.

Distractors should share some attributes with the correct object.

For example:

- small purple bag with yellow star — correct
- large purple bag
- small yellow bag
- purple ball with yellow star

This means successful selection requires genuine memory and visual discrimination.

---

# 22. Incorrect Retrieval

If the child selects the wrong object, they should be allowed to carry it back.

The game should not immediately declare it wrong.

On delivery, the character might respond:

> "Hmm... I think ours had a star on it."

The incorrect item then leaves naturally.

Examples:

- floats back
- bird carries it away
- magically disappears
- character puts it aside

The player can try again.

This preserves the meaningful memory requirement rather than revealing correctness immediately.

---

# 23. Prototype Challenge Progression

The first prototype should contain approximately three retrieval missions.

## Mission 1 — Recognition

Remember one highly distinctive object.

Purpose:

Understand the game loop.

## Mission 2 — Working Memory

Remember an object with two or more distinguishing attributes.

Purpose:

Test genuine recall and selective attention.

## Mission 3 — Reasoning

The character describes a property rather than showing the exact object.

Example:

> "We need something bigger than this apple."

The child must choose an appropriate object.

Purpose:

Test conceptual reasoning.

---

# 24. Prototype World Bridge

After one of the retrieval missions, the character should connect the game to the real world.

Example:

The character needs something large.

After retrieving it:

> "That's bigger than my hand! Can you find something near you that's bigger than your hand?"

The child leaves the screen and finds something.

The game trusts completion rather than requiring verification.

The character reacts when the child returns.

This tests whether World Bridges feel delightful or disruptive.

---

# 25. Prototype Free-Play Reward

The retrieved items should contribute toward an enjoyable activity.

Recommended example:

## Picnic / Cake Activity

After completing the retrieval missions, the character can use the gathered objects or ingredients.

The child can then freely:

- arrange items
- decorate
- interact with food
- feed the character
- create a cake or picnic
- trigger funny reactions

The activity must support immediate replay.

There is no requirement to "finish" permanently.

An **Again** interaction should be obvious.

---

# 26. Replayability

Favourite activities should be intentionally replayable.

The game must not assume:

> completed once = no longer useful.

The child may choose to:

- make ten cakes
- cross the river repeatedly
- intentionally jump on crocodiles
- repeat a dance
- repeatedly decorate the same thing

This behaviour is desirable.

Structured adventures and unstructured repetition should coexist.

---

# 27. Engagement Philosophy

The objective is not to minimise screen enjoyment.

The game should be extremely appealing.

However, engagement should primarily come from:

- curiosity
- mastery
- humour
- imagination
- agency
- creation
- relationships with characters
- satisfying interaction
- repetition
- discovery

rather than behavioural engagement tricks.

The game should provide natural moments where attention returns to the physical world.

---

# 28. Future Direction

The initial prototype does not need to implement these features, but the concept should remain compatible with them.

### Additional adventures

Examples:

- Floor Is Lava
- cake shop
- bedtime
- witches and potions
- mermaids
- dancing
- weather
- cooking
- getting dressed
- searching for Wormly

### Story creation

Parents or children may eventually describe a story and have it become an adventure.

### Personal characters

Parents may eventually add characters based on:

- family stories
- imaginary friends
- child drawings
- recurring games

### Responsive speech

The main character may eventually understand simple spoken responses and reply contextually.

Conversation should remain linked to the current game situation rather than becoming unrestricted chatbot interaction.

### Camera-supported interaction

Potential future activities include:

- showing the character a toy
- recognising objects
- copying poses
- dancing
- bringing drawings into the game

### Personalised story replay

The child's actions during an adventure could eventually be turned into a narrated story that can be replayed afterwards.

---

# 29. Prototype Success Questions

The prototype should primarily answer behavioural questions rather than technical ones.

Observe:

1. Can the child understand how to move the character without assistance?
2. Does she understand that the character needs her help?
3. Can she remember the target item across the river-crossing delay?
4. Does she understand how to pick up and return an object?
5. Are the crocodiles fun rather than frustrating?
6. Does she voluntarily repeat the crossing?
7. Does she enjoy deliberately experimenting or failing?
8. Can educational difficulty be introduced without reducing enjoyment?
9. Does the World Bridge make her excited to leave the screen temporarily?
10. Does she return to the game after the World Bridge?
11. Does she engage with the free-play activity repeatedly?
12. Does she talk about the character or adventure after playing?
13. Does she ask to play again later?

The answers to these questions should determine the next design iteration.

---

# 30. Definition of Success

The concept is successful if the child experiences it primarily as:

> **playing adventures with my character**

while the parent can accurately describe the same session as including:

- working memory
- reasoning
- attention
- spatial planning
- language
- physical activity
- creativity
- persistence
- real-world interaction

The game should satisfy both perspectives simultaneously.
